PART 00

The whole thing in one picture

There are two systems here, stacked. Keeping them separate in your head is the single most useful thing you can do before reading anything else.

The lower system is a cover-song identifier. You hand it a recording and a library of other recordings, and it returns a ranked list: most-likely-the-same-song first. Everything in Parts 1 through 8 is about how that works.

The upper system is an agent that builds and improves the lower one. Building a retrieval system involves dozens of choices — which features, which distance, which alignment, how many candidates to rerank. Normally a researcher makes those choices by running an experiment, reading the failures, and adjusting. The upper system does that loop itself. That is Part 9, and it is what the hackathon track actually asks for.

The one idea to hold onto

A cover song keeps the harmonic sequence — the pattern of chords and melody over time — and changes almost everything else. So the entire job is: build a representation that preserves that sequence and discards the rest, then compare sequences in a way that tolerates stretching.

PART 01

What makes a cover hard to find

Start with vocabulary, because the dataset uses it everywhere and it is genuinely clarifying.

A work is the abstract composition — the thing a songwriter wrote. A performance is one particular recording of it. In Da-TACOS these get IDs: W_163992 is a work, P_547131 is one performance of it. Every performance sharing a work ID forms a clique. Retrieval succeeds when a query pulls its clique-mates to the top.

Now, why is this hard? Consider what two recordings of the same work can differ in:

What changes Example What it breaks
Key Sung a fourth lower to fit a different vocal range Every absolute pitch is wrong
Tempo A ballad taken at double time Nothing lines up in time
Instrumentation Orchestral original, solo acoustic cover Timbre is unrecognisable
Structure Cover drops a verse, adds a solo, extends the outro Sections appear, vanish, reorder
Duration 3:10 versus 7:40 Sequences have different lengths
Production 1962 mono tape versus 2019 digital Spectral character differs wildly

Every one of those defeats the obvious approaches. Audio fingerprinting — the Shazam family — matches exact spectral landmarks, so it identifies the same recording heard through noise, and fails completely on a re-performance. Comparing raw waveforms or spectrograms fails for the same reason: they encode timbre and absolute pitch, both of which are exactly what a cover changes.

What actually survives is the relative harmonic movement. If the original goes "hold a chord for four beats, move up a fourth, come back down," a cover in a different key at a different tempo still does that. Different absolute notes, same shape.

Reframing the problem

The task is not "do these two recordings sound alike." It is "do these two sequences of harmonic states follow the same trajectory, allowing for a constant pitch offset and a non-constant time warp." That sentence is essentially the specification for the next four parts.

PART 02

From air pressure to numbers

Sound is a pressure wave. A microphone measures pressure many thousands of times per second, and each measurement is a number. At the standard 44,100 samples per second, three minutes of audio is about eight million numbers — a long one-dimensional array called the waveform.

The waveform is complete but useless for our purpose. It tells you the instantaneous pressure; it does not tell you which notes are sounding. Those two things are related the way a photograph's raw pixels relate to "there is a dog in this image" — the information is present but not in a form you can compare.

The Fourier idea

The foundational result is that any signal can be written as a sum of pure sine waves at different frequencies, each with its own amplitude. A Fourier transform recovers that recipe: hand it a chunk of waveform, and it returns how much energy sits at each frequency.

Applied to a whole song this is not useful, because a song's frequency content changes constantly — that is what music is. So instead we chop the waveform into short overlapping windows (typically ~20–90 milliseconds, short enough that the music is roughly stationary within one), Fourier-transform each window separately, and stack the results side by side. That is the Short-Time Fourier Transform, and the stacked result is a spectrogram: a matrix where one axis is time, the other is frequency, and each cell holds energy.

The trade-off you cannot escape

Short windows give you sharp timing but blurry frequency; long windows give sharp frequency but smeared timing. This is not an engineering limitation you can optimise away — it is the uncertainty principle applied to signals. Choosing the window length is choosing where you want your precision.

PART 03

Chroma: folding the piano into twelve

We need to throw away timbre and keep harmony. The tool for this is a piece of music theory that turns out to be computationally perfect.

Octave equivalence

Doubling a frequency raises a note by exactly one octave. 220 Hz is A3; 440 Hz is A4; 880 Hz is A5. Musically, all three are "A" — they serve the same harmonic function, and a listener hears them as the same note in different registers. Western music divides the octave into twelve semitones, so there are exactly twelve pitch classes: C, C♯, D, D♯, E, F, F♯, G, G♯, A, A♯, B.

So: take the spectrogram, and for each frame, sum all the energy belonging to every C (across every octave) into one bucket, all the energy belonging to every C♯ into another, and so on. Twelve buckets. That twelve-dimensional vector is a chroma vector, and the sequence of them is a chromagram.

HPCP, and why Da-TACOS ships it pre-computed

HPCP — Harmonic Pitch Class Profile — is a refined chroma. It weights each spectral peak's harmonics, corrects for instruments tuned slightly off A440, and normalises per frame. For cover detection it consistently beats naive chroma, which is why it is the standard.

Crucially, Da-TACOS ships HPCP features already extracted. You never touch audio. That is a large practical gift: the raw audio would be hundreds of gigabytes and legally messy, whereas the features are tractable and redistributable. Your pipeline starts at "load a matrix," not "decode an MP3."

What you have after Part 3

Every track is now an (n_frames, 12) matrix of non-negative numbers. Two covers in the same key at the same tempo would now have very similar matrices. The remaining two problems — different key, different tempo — are Parts 4 and 5.

PART 04

The key problem and twelve rotations

Here is where the twelve-bin choice pays off spectacularly.

Transposing music up by one semitone maps every C to C♯, every C♯ to D, every B back around to C. In chroma space, that is not a complicated transformation — it is a circular shift of the twelve bins. A song played three semitones higher has an identical chroma matrix with its columns rotated by three.

And since there are only twelve possible transpositions, you can simply try all of them and keep the best. Twelve is a small number; exhaustive search is free.

The Optimal Transposition Index

Trying all twelve rotations of the full matrix during alignment would be twelve times the work. The standard shortcut, from Serrà et al. (2008), is to estimate the shift once from summary statistics: average each track's chroma over all frames to get a single twelve-dimensional "key profile," then find the rotation that maximises the dot product between the two profiles. That rotation is the Optimal Transposition Index.

A bug we actually hit

NumPy's np.roll(a, k) sets output[j] = input[(j − k) % n]. So if a candidate was produced by rolling the query by +5, recovering the query means rolling the candidate by −5, which is 7 in mod-12 arithmetic. Our first unit test asserted the OTI would come back as 5, and it failed with 7 — the test was wrong, not the implementation. Whenever you write rotation code, pin the direction down with a test that rotates a known vector and checks specific bins, never by assuming.

src/cover_retrieval/alignment/transposition.py

def optimal_transposition_index(chroma_a, chroma_b):
    mean_a = chroma_a.mean(axis=0)
    mean_b = chroma_b.mean(axis=0)

    best_shift, best_score = 0, -np.inf
    for shift in range(12):
        rotated = np.roll(mean_b, shift)
        score = float(np.dot(mean_a, rotated))
        if score > best_score:
            best_score, best_shift = score, shift
    return best_shift

Twelve rotations, one dot product each, one argmax. The entire key-invariance problem — which sounds like it should be hard — costs about ten lines because chroma was the right representation.

PART 05

Comparing sequences that drift

Key is solved. Time is not. A cover might be slower, might linger on the chorus, might skip the second verse entirely. Frame 400 of the query could correspond to frame 700 of the candidate — and frame 401 to frame 703, because the tempo ratio is not even constant.

The cross-similarity matrix

Before aligning, look at all the evidence at once. Build a matrix where entry (i, j) is the distance between query frame i and candidate frame j. With normalised chroma vectors, cosine distance works: 1 − cos θ, zero for identical harmonic content, one for orthogonal.

Dynamic time warping

DTW finds the cheapest monotonic path through that matrix. Monotonic matters: you may stretch time, but you may not run backwards, because music has direction.

The trick is that you never enumerate paths — there are exponentially many. Instead you build up the answer cell by cell. Define D(i, j) as the cost of the best path that ends at cell (i, j). To arrive at (i, j) you must have come from one of three neighbours, so:

D(i, j) = c(i, j) + min{ D(i−1, j),   D(i−1, j−1),   D(i, j−1) } where c(i, j) is the raw cost of matching query frame i to candidate frame j. Diagonal means both advance together; the other two mean one waits while the other moves — that is a time stretch.

src/cover_retrieval/alignment/dtw.py

acc = np.full((n_q, n_c), np.inf)
acc[0, :] = cost[0, :]          # free start anywhere along the candidate

for i in range(1, n_q):
    for j in range(n_c):
        candidates = [acc[i - 1, j]]
        if j > 0:
            candidates.append(acc[i - 1, j - 1])
            candidates.append(acc[i, j - 1])
        acc[i, j] = cost[i, j] + min(candidates)

end_index = int(np.argmin(acc[-1, :]))   # best ending column
best_cost = float(acc[-1, end_index])

One final detail: we divide the final cost by the query length. Without that, longer queries accumulate more cost simply for being longer, and the ranking would be dominated by duration rather than similarity. Normalising makes scores comparable across pairs.

Be precise about which classical method you built

There are two related classical approaches, and papers conflate them. Serrà et al. (2008) binarise the cross-similarity matrix and run a Smith–Waterman-style local alignment (the Qmax/Dmax family). Müller's FMP tradition uses subsequence DTW on the raw cost matrix. We implemented the second. Both are defensible; describing yours as "Serrà 2008" when it is the FMP formulation would be a real accuracy problem in a write-up.

PART 06

Ranking, and measuring honestly

Now the machinery runs: for one query, align it against every candidate, collect scores, sort ascending. What comes out is a ranked list. The question is how to put a number on whether that list is good.

Accuracy is the wrong tool. If a query has 4 true covers among 1,000 candidates, a system that returns "not a cover" for everything is 99.6% accurate and completely useless. What matters is where in the ranking the true covers land.

Average Precision, built up from scratch

Precision@k is: of the top k results, what fraction are correct. Average Precision takes precision@k measured at each rank where a correct item appears, and averages those values. Rewarding early hits falls out naturally, because a hit at rank 1 is measured when precision is still high.

The rest follow quickly:

Metric Definition What it tells you
MAP mean of AP over all queries Overall ranking quality; the headline number in this field
MRR mean of 1 / rank of first hit How fast a user finds one correct answer
Recall@1 fraction of true covers at rank 1 Strict top-hit performance
Recall@10 fraction found within top 10 Realistic "user scans a page" performance
Recall@100 fraction found within top 100 Whether a cheap first stage is safe to rerank

That last row matters more than it looks — it is the number that licenses the hybrid design in Part 8. If a fast method reliably puts the true cover somewhere in its top 100, an expensive method only ever has to examine 100 candidates.

Two protocol rules you must not get wrong

Exclude self-matches. A track is trivially its own best match; leaving it in inflates every metric. Our rank_candidates skips any candidate whose PID equals the query's.

Do not touch the benchmark while developing. Tune on a development split; run the benchmark once, at the end. Every peek at test data that informs a decision leaks information and quietly converts your result into an overfit number you cannot defend.

PART 07

Learning the representation

The classical pipeline works, and it has a fatal practical flaw: cost. Aligning one pair costs O(n×m) in frames — for two four-minute tracks, millions of cell updates. Searching 15,000 candidates means 15,000 alignments per query. It does not scale.

There is also a quality ceiling. Every design choice — cosine distance, mean-profile OTI, these three DTW moves — is a hand-made guess about what makes covers similar. Reasonable guesses, but guesses.

The reframing: learn a map to a shared space

Instead of comparing sequences pairwise, learn a function that reads a whole track and emits a single fixed-length vector — say 256 numbers — such that covers of the same work land close together and unrelated songs land far apart. Then comparing two tracks is one dot product rather than a dynamic programme.

You do not tell the network what features to use. You only tell it, through the loss, what the geometry must look like.

Triplet loss

Show the network three tracks: an anchor, a positive (same clique), and a negative (different clique). Demand that the anchor sit closer to the positive than to the negative, by at least a margin.

L = max( 0,   d(a, p) − d(a, n) + margin ) If the positive is already closer than the negative by more than the margin, the loss is exactly zero and that triplet contributes no gradient. Only triplets the network currently gets wrong — or gets right too narrowly — push the weights.
Mining is most of the difficulty

Randomly sampled triplets are almost all trivially satisfied within an epoch or two, producing zero gradient and stalled training. You need hard negatives — different-work tracks the model currently thinks are close. Getting mining right matters more to final accuracy than the choice of encoder architecture, and it is the single most common reason a triplet model underperforms.

Why a TCN for the encoder

The encoder has to turn a variable-length chroma sequence into one fixed vector. A Temporal Convolutional Network is a good first choice: stacked 1-D convolutions with dilation — gaps between the samples each filter looks at — doubling per layer, so the receptive field grows exponentially with depth while cost grows linearly. Ten layers see thousands of frames.

Compared with the alternatives: RNNs process frames sequentially and cannot parallelise across time, making them slow on long sequences. Transformers are strong but scale quadratically with sequence length and need far more data than Da-TACOS provides. A TCN parallelises like a CNN and stays cheap. Start there; justify anything fancier with an ablation.

FAISS: search once the vectors exist

With every track a 256-dimensional vector, retrieval becomes nearest-neighbour search. FAISS with IndexFlatIP does exact inner-product search; L2-normalise your vectors first and inner product equals cosine similarity. Searching 15,000 vectors is milliseconds. Start exact — approximate indices add a recall/speed knob you should only introduce once exact search is genuinely the bottleneck.

Property Classical DTW Learned embedding
Cost per pair O(n × m) O(d), one dot product
Training needed None Substantial — labelled cliques, GPU time
Interpretability High — you can plot the alignment path Low — 256 opaque numbers
Handles structural edits Well, by construction Only if training data contained them
Scales to 15,000 candidates No Yes

Notice these are not ranked — they are complementary. That observation is the whole of Part 8.

PART 08

The hybrid system

The classical method is precise but cannot scale. The learned method scales but is blurrier about fine structural detail. So use each where it is strong: embeddings to cheaply narrow 15,000 candidates to 100, DTW to carefully reorder those 100.

This also gives each stage a distinct metric to optimise, which is a genuinely useful engineering property: stage one is judged purely on whether truth survives into the shortlist, stage two purely on ordering. Two clean objectives instead of one muddy one.

PART 09

The agent layer

Step back and count the decisions made in Parts 3 through 8. Chroma or HPCP. Cosine or Euclidean. Mean-profile OTI or all-twelve-rotations. Subsequence DTW or local alignment. Shortlist of 50, 100, or 500. Embedding dimension. Triplet margin. Mining strategy.

Dozens of knobs, and the usual way to set them is: pick a value, run the eval, read which queries failed, form a hypothesis, change one thing, run again. That loop is skilled work, but it is also mechanical — and mechanisable.

What Agent Forge does

The system holds a configuration, runs it, scores it, clusters the failures, and asks a language model: given these metrics and this failure profile, what one change should we make next? Then it applies that change and runs again, keeping a log of every round.

The thing to get right: what counts as an "architecture"

Most agent frameworks treat "improving an agent" as "editing a system prompt." That framing does not fit here, because the thing being improved is a signal-processing and retrieval pipeline, not a chatbot. The decision space is a configuration:

Knob Options the agent chooses among Failure mode it addresses
Transposition Mean-profile OTI, all-12 rotations, per-segment OTI Covers that modulate key mid-song
Alignment Subsequence DTW, local alignment, banded DTW Structural edits, extreme tempo ratios
Distance Cosine, Euclidean, binarised similarity Noisy or sparse chroma frames
Shortlist size 50 / 100 / 500 Recall lost before reranking; runtime cost
Encoder None, TCN, TCN + hybrid rerank Scale limits; ceiling on hand-made features

Framed this way, the three systems in this document — classical, learned, hybrid — are three points in one configuration space, and the agent's job is to navigate it using evidence rather than intuition.

The strategic risk, stated plainly

Track 1 rewards demonstrating this "across multiple distinct domains." Committing entirely to cover-song retrieval is a bet that depth beats breadth. The defensible pitch is that classical, learned, and hybrid are genuinely different architectures with different cost/accuracy profiles — but that argument has to be made explicitly in the submission, because a judge reading the rubric literally will not make it for you. Consider whether a second, cheap domain is worth a few hours as insurance.

What to read, in what order

Resist reading everything up front. Each item below unlocks exactly the next thing you need to build.

  1. Da-TACOS paper (Yesiler et al., 2019), sections 3 and 5 — read this before writing manifest code, because it defines cliques, distractors, and the evaluation protocol you must not violate.
  2. FMP Chapter 3 (Müller) — chroma features and DTW, with runnable notebooks. This is the single best source for the classical baseline, and you should execute it rather than read it.
  3. Serrà et al., 2008 — chroma binary similarity and local alignment. Read after your DTW works, so you can see precisely how the alternative differs.
  4. Stanford IR book, evaluation chapter — AP, MAP, reciprocal rank. Short, and it will stop you misreporting your own numbers.
  5. Doras & Peeters, 2020 — prototypical triplet loss for cover detection. Read only once the classical baseline produces a real number to beat.
  6. Bai, Kolter & Koltun, 2018 — the TCN paper. Justifies the encoder choice before you write it.
The habit that matters most

Hand-calculate before you trust. Every metric in this project was verified against a toy example computed on paper before it ever touched real data — and the one bug we found was caught exactly that way. On a project where the output is a number you will put in a submission, a metric you have not personally verified is a liability, not an asset.
