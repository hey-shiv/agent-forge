How to read this

Section 01

This document is the plan for your textbook, not the textbook. You asked for the contents, the dependency map, difficulty ratings, projects, the capstone architecture, the glossary plan, and the quiz strategy — all of that is here. Then we stop, and you tell me to start Chapter 0.

Three things shaped the structure beyond what you specified.

The 46-hour reality. Your proposed sixteen parts are the right topics, but sequenced for a semester. I kept the topics and added a triage layer on top, so you always know what is urgent versus what is thorough.

Concepts arrive when a problem demands them. You asked never to be handed terminology for its own sake. So the chapter titles below are mostly phrased as problems or questions, not as topic labels — "Why a model can't touch the world" rather than "Tool calling." The term appears in the chapter, after you've felt the gap it fills.

Four recurring examples, escalating. A calculator agent, customer-support routing, invoice extraction, and cover-song retrieval. Each one reappears every few parts, slightly harder each time, so you're never learning a new domain and a new concept simultaneously.

The 46-hour path

Section 02

If you read nothing else, read these twenty-two chapters in this order. They are scattered across the full curriculum, but pulled together they form a complete, if compressed, arc: what an LLM is, how it becomes an agent, how you measure one, how you diagnose one, and what the hackathon is actually asking you to build.

Rough budget: these average 15–20 minutes each with the exercises skipped, so roughly six to seven hours of reading. That fits comfortably before Saturday, leaving Friday for the first prototype.

A judgement call you should make consciously

Thirty hours is enough to build a working Automated Agent Engineering system on simple domains — support routing, invoice extraction, document QA. Part XIII is written on that assumption.

Dependency map

Section 03

This is the whole field as one connected system. The spine down the middle is the path you must walk in order — each node genuinely requires the one above it. Branches to the right are things that hang off a spine concept but don't block progress.

Notice where the spine turns: everything above the agent loop is about making one model call useful. Everything below it is about making many calls reliable. That turn is the real subject of this book.

Capstone architecture

Section 06

This is where the whole book lands. Everything you learn is a component in this diagram. Right now most of the boxes will mean nothing to you — that's expected, and it's the point. By Chapter 78 you'll be able to draw this from memory and argue about the design of every box.

The one thing worth noticing today: the loop back at the bottom. That arrow is the entire submission. A system that runs an agent once is a demo; a system that runs it, learns something specific from the failures, changes the right thing, and runs it again is what the track is asking for.

Glossary plan

Section 07

The glossary grows as you read rather than arriving pre-built, because a definition you meet before the problem it solves is just noise. Every term gets five fields, in this order — deliberately the same order concepts are taught in.

FieldPurpose
Plain meaningOne sentence, no jargon, the version you'd tell a friend
Technical definitionThe version you'd write in a paper
Tiny exampleThree lines or fewer, concrete
Why it existsThe problem that forced someone to invent it
Related termsLinks up and down the dependency map

Roughly 120 terms by the end, introduced at a rate of two to four per chapter. Terms are added only in the chapter where the concept is earned — you will never meet a glossary entry before the chapter that motivates it. A full alphabetised index appears at the back, cross-referenced by chapter.

Seed terms from the first three parts, to give you the flavour: token, context window, temperature, system prompt, schema, tool call, agent loop, state, trajectory, orchestration.

Quiz and exercise strategy

Section 08

Questions escalate in kind, not just in difficulty. The type of question you're asked tells you what level you're operating at — and by Part X you should notice the questions have quietly become the same ones a hackathon judge would ask.

PartsQuestion typeExample
I–IIRecall and recognition "What does the model actually receive when you call it?"
III–IVPrediction "This agent has no loop limit. What happens on a tool that always errors?"
V–VIDiagnosis "Accuracy is fine but latency tripled. Which component would you look at first?"
VII–VIIIJudgement "Is this a prompt problem or an architecture problem? Defend your answer."
IX–XIDesign "Your improvement loop plateaued at round 3. What are three possible causes?"
XII–XVAdversarial / research "A judge says your system is hardcoded. What evidence disproves that?"

Each chapter closes with three to five questions and one practical exercise. Answers are given, but placed at the end of the part rather than the chapter — the gap is deliberate, because retrieving an answer you half-remember is what actually builds the model. Every part also ends with one challenge problem that has no clean answer, only better and worse arguments.
